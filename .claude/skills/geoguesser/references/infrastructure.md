# Road infrastructure

The highest-yield category, because almost every round is on a road. Source for most of
this is plonkit.net's per-country pages. Confidence is high unless marked.

Read in this order: **bollard -> road lines -> pole -> guardrail -> signs -> plate.**

---

## Bollards

The single best clue in the game. Many countries are a one-look ID.

### Nordics and Baltics

| Country | Bollard |
|---|---|
| Norway | Thin, curved rectangular; reflector sits inside a **black parallelogram** |
| Sweden | Black-and-white; grey reflector, orange near intersections. Wedge = south; cylindrical with a horizontal black band = west coast / Värmland / south Dalarna; flat with a black rectangle = north |
| Finland | Long black-and-white rectangular; rectangular white front reflector, two dots on the back; diagonal black strip |
| Iceland | **Yellow**, white reflector. Unique, ubiquitous on rural roads |
| Estonia | Round, baton-like; rectangular front reflector, two circular back reflectors (white or orange) |
| Latvia | Thin, slightly curved; rectangular front plus two circular back reflectors; angled black strip; numbers below the front reflector |
| Lithuania | Wedge-shaped; orange front, white back, both rectangular |

### Western and Central Europe

| Country | Bollard |
|---|---|
| Germany | Black-and-white, white/light-grey reflectors (orange at intersections), road-number plates, blue side attachments. Extremely dense, roughly every 50 m |
| Luxembourg | Near-identical to Germany but **3 bolts** where Germany has 2 |
| France | Round white with two reflective indented bands, unique. Also green and yellow variants. A wedge type with wide rectangular white reflectors front and back is also France-only |
| Belgium | White with a yellow rectangular front reflector, white back. Second type is dark brown with two red stripes at the top (also seen in the Netherlands) |
| Netherlands | Simple white, red reflector. Rare French-style round ones exist but the red does not fully encircle |
| Switzerland | Black-and-white, white/grey reflectors, wedge or rounded-cylindrical. Valais canton uses thick wooden square or cylindrical posts |
| Liechtenstein | Near-identical to Switzerland, round |
| Denmark | White with a yellow reflector and a **dark orange stripe**, unique to the country. A green variant exists |
| Ireland | Green-and-white. Decisive in a UK/Ireland coin flip |
| UK | Rounded, red front / white back reflector, black base and top with a white middle stripe. Scotland uses round bulky ones with a red-and-white wrap-around reflector |

### Southern Europe

| Country | Bollard |
|---|---|
| Spain | Yellow-orange front reflector, two white dots on the back, hollow. Aragón: flat white stick, grey reflector both sides. Lleida/Tarragona and eastern Canaries: square orange reflector, white back. Extremadura: green tops. Las Palmas province has its own design |
| Portugal | Wedge with a thin white top, or flat with a wide reflector. Mostly white reflectors, some orange, darker than Spain's yellow |
| Greece | Wedge, almost-square reflectors, red front / white back |
| Croatia | White with a black rectangle, red or white reflector, triangular from above with sharper edges than Hungary or Lithuania |
| Hungary | Black-and-white wedge, red front / white back reflector; blue also common |
| Czechia and Slovakia | Identical: orange double reflectors front, single white back. Only these two use this design. Slovakia is migrating to a Hungary-style wedge |
| Poland | Red strip **wraps fully around**; red front reflector, white back, sometimes numbered. Same design appears in Ukraine's Zakarpattia Oblast |
| Romania | Thin white with a red vertical strip near the top. Bollards are relatively rare here |
| Turkey | Similar to Romania. Mostly rectangular plain white with a red front reflector; a rare yellow variant in snow country |

### Russia and the east

Three main types: thin stick-mounted (east, from Omsk onward), black-top with a red
vertical reflector, and German-style. Very dense near intersections.

### Americas

| Country | Bollard |
|---|---|
| Mexico | Small cylindrical white, black base, sometimes a yellow reflector; a flat variant too. Unique to Mexico. Nayarit adds a red band near the top. Jalisco has a crown and the word "Jalisco", the only Mexican bollard with writing |
| Chile | Spanish-style, white front / yellow-orange back reflector, not very common. Temporary orange ones with two white stripes also appear |
| Canada | Manitoba: black/grey painted, orange stripe below grey. BC: black-and-white, thin curved, wider black-band spacing than Alberta. NWT: black-and-white cylindrical, two thick black bands. New Brunswick: flat yellow on the Trans-Canada |
| USA | Heavily state-coded, see `regionguessing.md` |

### Oceania

| Country | Bollard |
|---|---|
| Australia | White, red front reflector, grey back. Short reflectors = QLD/SA. Brown paint at base = WA. Black-and-white stripes = WA, unique. Black-and-yellow = QLD, unique |
| New Zealand | Red strip wraps around the top and partway around the front, thin yellow or white reflector. **Unique to NZ** and the clean split from Australia, whose red reflector stays in the middle rather than wrapping. Canterbury/Southland green-stripe and Wellington black-stripe variants |

### Asia

| Country | Bollard |
|---|---|
| Japan | White with circular reflector(s) on top, sometimes black, sometimes carrying kanji place names. Kyushu/Yamaguchi: flat white, orange reflectors one side. Tohoku: thick white, octagonal reflector plus yellow stripe. Akita: two yellow stripes. Toyama: extendable, one reflector, red stripe. Okayama: octagonal reflector, red stripe, sloped base. Aomori: extendable, two yellow stripes |
| South Korea | Black-and-white wedge, round yellow front reflector, grey back |
| Thailand | Square, pointed top, black-and-white stripes (shared family with Cambodia, Laos, Indonesia). Chiang Mai / Mae Hong Son: black with three large white reflectors |
| Malaysia | Black/white/grey/red combination, several layouts but a consistent palette |
| Indonesia | Black-and-white square or circular, or yellow-and-black sometimes with a red reflector. UK-style bollards appear in Kalimantan. Central Sulawesi has a unique black/white/yellow tri-band |

**Gaps:** Italy and India bollards were not captured; check plonkit directly if a round
turns on one.

## Road lines

**The continental split, checked first:** yellow centre lines mean the Americas (plus
Norway, Finland, parts of Greece). White-only or mixed means everywhere else.

### Americas

- **USA** — yellow centre, white outer, **double yellow most common**. A larger gap
  between the two yellow lines means TX/AR/LA/MS/OR. A black stripe between them means
  California. CA and Kentucky sometimes drop the centre line entirely. Kentucky also has
  long rumble-strip cuts on the outer edge.
- **Canada** — single yellow centre is more common nationally, though double yellow is
  still common (most in Nova Scotia, then Québec and PEI; Ontario prefers single).
  Saskatchewan puts white dashes between the yellow centre lines.
- **Mexico** — white solid outer plus a single unbroken yellow centre. Black tar patching
  around the yellow line means Sinaloa, Durango or Guerrero.
- **Brazil** — double yellow centre with white outer, occasionally single dashed yellow.
- **Argentina** — a real mix: dashed white, double yellow, or blends, plus a sizeable
  minority of all-white roads. Do not lean on yellow here.
- **Chile** — distinctive: roads are either **all white** or **all yellow**, rare in the
  Americas. Triple yellow lines appear in specific national parks.
- **Uruguay** — triple lines: double yellow with white dashes between. *Medium confidence,
  corroborated across community sources but not from a primary page.*

### Europe (mostly white; the discriminators are dash length, doubling, and absence)

- **Norway** — orange-tinted yellow centre on larger roads; long-dashed white outer on
  smaller ones.
- **Sweden** — white only, never yellow centre; short outer dashes, shorter than Norway's.
- **Denmark** — all white, very common double centre lines, distinctive small
  white-square-segmented outer lines.
- **Poland** — all white; **double centre lines very common**, a good Poland tell.
- **Greece** — solid white double centre lines with a faded, worn look. More yellow road
  lines than any other southern European country.
- **Czechia** — solid outer lines, frequently **no centre line at all**.
- **Hungary** — all-white road lines, but bicycle-lane lines are yellow.
- **Romania vs Bulgaria** — Romanian centre lines are noticeably **thicker**. Direct.
- **Belgium** — all white, often flanked by bike lanes on both sides.
- **Netherlands** — all white, dashed outside lines common; rare green centre lines exist.
- **UK** — long dashes **with cat's-eye reflectors in the gaps**, near-unique. Double
  yellow lines along the road edge (no parking) are common in the UK and Malta, rare
  elsewhere in Europe.
- **France** — long, widely spaced outer dashes. A centre-dash style where each dash is
  split into two offset blocks is diagnostic for France.
- **Spain** — rural roads sometimes get dashed outer lines, near-absent in Portugal and
  Italy. Useful for splitting those three.
- **Italy** — solid white outer lines with **no centre line at all** is common.

### Asia, Africa, Oceania

- **Malaysia** — **double white** centre lines on two-lane roads, the only country in SE
  Asia doing this. Centre lines are always white, never yellow.
- **Indonesia** — white or yellow centre (yellow increasingly common on Gen 4+, rarer on
  Gen 3), outer always white.
- **Philippines** — yellow and white combined in the centre, not seen elsewhere in East Asia.
- **Thailand** — yellow centre lines, solid or dashed.
- **Turkey** — inconsistent, mostly white but yellow or mixed is common. Four-lane divided
  highways with no physical divider, just double centre lines, concentrate in the east.
- **South Africa** — single **yellow outer** lines plus a white centre. Shared with
  Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho and Namibia, so this is a southern-Africa tell rather than a
  South Africa one. A rare triple centre line (2 solid outside, 1 dashed inside) is SA-only.
- **New Zealand** — white outer normally, yellow dashed outer in populated areas. Centre is
  a single dashed white or an unbroken double yellow.
- **Australia** — almost exclusively white. Yellow is the exception and localises to alpine
  regions. So in an AU/NZ/ZA three-way, yellow pushes you to South Africa (outer) or New
  Zealand (centre).
- **Russia** — dashed outer lines are characteristic of Kursk Oblast. Concrete-block road
  surfaces get more common heading north into Komi and the Nenets districts.

## Utility poles

- **Netherlands** — essentially **no street-level utility poles at all**, only larger
  transmission lines. An absence-based tell, and Belgium next door uses them commonly.
- **Denmark** — street-level poles are rare; big overhead pylons near highways instead.
- **Germany** — wide variety of pole tops (horizontal bar with uneven insulators, triangle
  tops, T-tops). Wooden poles carry rectangular white stickers. Round concrete poles are
  near-unique to the former East.
- **France** — concrete "ladder" or "waffle" poles with stepped indents (also common in
  Spain). Small **blue rectangular plates** are unique to France. Diamond-shaped pole tops
  with 3 wires are common, also found in Spain.
- **Belgium** — mostly square concrete with tiny vertical holes and small metal wrappings.
  Traffic-light poles: yellow-black stripes are unique to Flanders, red-white in Wallonia
  and Brussels.
- **Czechia and Slovakia** — wide round concrete poles, often paired; short metal bars
  holding insulators; upside-down-triangle support bars (also Germany, Albania, Serbia,
  Romania).
- **Poland / Hungary / Romania** — all use perforated "holey" concrete poles. The
  discriminator is hole placement: Polish holes are thin and stop short of the ground;
  Hungarian holes are wider and bulkier; Romanian holes reach the ground and are either
  very wide or very tall.
- **Nordics** — mostly wooden with a small black metal cap. The three-way split is the
  snow-pole reflector: Norway thin and near the middle, Sweden wide and higher up, Finland
  thin and right at the top with a single band. Lamp mounting: Sweden and Finland
  double-bolt (Sweden lower with non-straight arms, Finland at the top and straighter),
  Norway uses one thick bolt.
- **Baltics** — Latvia: hook-shaped insulators alternating left-right. Lithuania: square
  concrete with a diagonal support beam, trident insulator arrangement. Estonia: least
  consistent, a mix of round wooden and square concrete.
- **Russia / Ukraine / ex-USSR** — square concrete poles nearly universal (also Lithuania,
  Estonia, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan). Concrete support blocks at the base are common
  across Central and Eastern Europe. White-painted pole and tree bottoms are common in
  Romania, Ukraine and parts of Russia.
- **Japan** — round concrete with screw-like extensions. Region is readable from plates,
  guy-wires and reflector-pad stripe patterns; see `regionguessing.md` for the full
  transformer-shape-by-region key, which is essentially a fingerprint.
- **South Korea vs Taiwan** — near-identical black-and-yellow diagonal pole-bottom stripes.
  Korea's are yellow and stop short of the ground; Taiwan's are more orange and reach it.
- **India** — overwhelmingly concrete or metal; wooden poles are rare, which is a strong
  subcontinental tell on its own. Trident-top concrete is the baseline, with dense
  state-level variation.
- **Brazil** — enormous state-level variety in insulator shape and colour, crossbar
  asymmetry, and pole-ID paint. See `regionguessing.md`.
- **Mexico** — octagonal concrete poles nationally (shared with Colombia). Acorn-shaped
  white insulators concentrate in the coastal states.
- **South Africa** — the "bird pole" (1 to 5 horizontal bars, thin white insulators) is the
  national baseline, with province-level variants on top.
- **Australia** — the **Stobie pole** (steel and concrete composite) is unique to South
  Australia, with small numbers bleeding into Tasmania and Broken Hill.
- **USA** — California pole bases carry 3 yellow horizontal stripes (except Modoc and
  Lassen counties). PacifiCorp's thick yellow horizontal plates mark OR, eastern ID, UT and
  WY, less often CA and WA.

## Guardrails

Profile shape plus reflector colour is the core European discriminator. A-type has a
rounded top rail; B-type is squared and boxier.

- **Yellow reflectors** — Spain, Belgium, Andorra, Gibraltar, and most of Latin America.
- **Red reflectors** — Italy, Portugal, Lithuania (orange specifically), Latvia (white or
  red), Poland (circular red on B-type).
- **No reflectors** — Estonia, Denmark, France (mostly), Czechia and Slovakia.
- **B-type countries** — Croatia, Denmark, Poland, Germany (B-type dominant, A-type in the
  north-west, Rhineland-Palatinate and parts of Bavaria), Czechia and Slovakia (a special
  extra-wide B-type unique to those two).
- **A-type countries** — most of the rest of Europe, including France (occasional thin
  B-type on northern highways), Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Portugal, Spain.
- **Both types together** — Turkey, Montenegro, and Israel/West Bank. Seeing a mix narrows
  hard.
- **Ceuta** — black-and-white, unique within Spain.
- **Russia and Ukraine** — black-and-white striped guardrails, kerbs and bridge barriers.
- **Canada** — BC leans on concrete barriers. Ontario has the classic wooden-post
  three-wire guardrail. Newfoundland uses pyramid-topped wooden posts.
- **USA** — square-top guardrails (long bar, square support legs) concentrate in New York
  and Wyoming.
- **Argentina vs Uruguay** — both use yellow/red rectangular reflectors; the support-post
  shape is the split.
- **Japan** — plain white nationally. Green bands mean Oita, Miyazaki or Kyushu generally.
  Yellow means Yamaguchi. Chevron stickers mean Hokkaido. Red-and-yellow striped end-caps
  mean Chugoku except Yamaguchi.

## Signs and markers

### Chevrons (arrow on background)

| Scheme | Where |
|---|---|
| White on black / black on yellow | Australia (NZ shares the colours but adds a thin black border and often a speed number) |
| Yellow and black | Most of Canada; Québec is the outlier at red-and-white |
| Yellow background, black arrow | Chile (vs Argentina's white background, red arrow) |
| **Yellow arrows on black** | Brazil, inverted from most of South America's black-on-yellow |
| White arrows on blue | France and Spain only, in Europe. Spain uses exactly 2 or 4 arrows; France ranges 1 to 5 |
| White on black | Italy, Spain, Greece, Albania |
| White on red / red on yellow | Austria (Germany is mostly red-on-white, rarely red-on-yellow) |

### Speed limits and wording

- **USA** — "Speed Limit" on two lines, mph, speeds ending in 5 more common. Oregon
  uniquely just says "Speed."
- **Canada** — "Maximum" on one line, km/h, generally higher numbers.
- US signs are consistently more text-heavy than Canadian equivalents: literal "YIELD",
  "RAILROAD CROSSING", "ONE WAY" where Canada's are blank. Canada uses diamond-shaped
  T-intersection signs where the US uses rectangular.

### Kilometre markers

- **Germany** — blue rectangle with a white border on the Autobahn; white with a black
  border on B-roads and local roads; triangular markers on smaller roads.
- **France** — yellow or red semicircle atop a white rectangle; square marker with a red
  top on major highways.
- **Italy** — white square, a Roman numeral (hundreds of metres) above an Arabic numeral
  (km). The road-number prefix gives the class: SS state, SR regional, SP provincial.
- **Czechia vs Slovakia** — Czech markers have a yellow outline outside the black frame and
  no horizontal middle line; Slovak markers have the middle line and no yellow outline.
- **Austria** — blue markers, white numbers, one decimal with a comma. Slovenia uses white;
  Germany uses a thin white outline.

### National quirks worth memorising

- **Germany** — "Einbahnstraße" for one-way (Austria says "EINBAHN"). Black or dark-grey
  sign backs, shared rarely with Italy, Romania and Albania. Yellow metallic-framed
  town-entry signs.
- **Austria** — blue-outlined direction signs in a distinctive font. The pedestrian-crossing
  sign uses two dashed lines instead of zebra stripes, unique in the region. Yellow-green
  circular "H" bus-stop signs, shared only with Germany.
- **Pedestrian-crossing stripe counts** — Germany 5 plus a belt; Italy 5, no belt; France 5
  with a disconnected head; Hungary 5 plus a belt (a 4-stripe variant with a bag exists);
  Belgium dotted lines and a square head; **Spain 8, the highest in Europe and near-unique**;
  South Korea uses a pentagonal blue sign instead of stripes.
- **USA vs Canada** — green exit signs with curved corners in the US, square corners in
  Canada.
- **France** — strong regional sign-colour sub-meta, see `regionguessing.md`.
- **UK** — almost no dedicated pedestrian-crossing signs. Instead, black-and-white striped
  poles with a yellow globe on top (Belisha beacons).

## Licence plates

- **Long plate with a blue EU strip on the left** — the European default: Austria, Ireland,
  Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Norway (non-EU, same format), Sweden (taxis get yellow),
  Turkey, Ukraine (its blue-and-yellow flag-coloured strip is its own tell).
- **Long and white with no strip** — Switzerland; **Russia** (fully white, no strip at all,
  a clean giveaway against its blue-strip neighbours); Iceland (white, blue characters).
  Croatia is mixed, and the ratio shifts by Street View generation.
- **Two blue strips, both sides** — Italy (front plate distinctly short) and France (modern
  plates; the left strip is more visible). Older French plates lack the right strip and the
  rear plate is yellow.
- **Yellow plates** — Netherlands (standard; taxis are blue), Luxembourg, UK (**rear only**,
  and UK plates often lack the blue strip), Laos (all regular vehicles).
- **Black plates with white sections showing through the blur** — Indonesia has **3** white
  sections, Malaysia has **2**. A clean discriminator.
- **Front plate present or absent** — USA: roughly half of states do not require one.
  Canada: province-dependent. Mexico requires them universally with state-specific designs.
- **Argentina vs Brazil** — Argentina's older black plate has a black dot in the middle, and
  that is Argentina-exclusive. Both have a blue bar or stripe on the newer white plates, so
  that alone does not separate them.
- **Regionally codable** — Russia (two-digit region code on the right, see
  `regionguessing.md`), Ukraine (first two letters are the oblast, non-alphabetical),
  Mexico, US and Canada (full state/province redesigns), India (two-letter state prefix).
- **Colour-coded by use** — yellow taxi plates in Greece and Hungary; yellow commercial
  plates across Thailand; green EV plates in India and Norway.
- **Short plate = Americas, long plate = Europe and much of Asia** is itself a first-order
  continental cut.

## Crossings, curbs, speed bumps

- **Yellow-painted pedestrian crossings** — near-exclusive to Switzerland and Liechtenstein
  in Europe. Russia uses alternating white-and-yellow crossing stripes.
- **Speed bumps** — the Netherlands marks them with alternating long and short white
  stripes, recognisable on both brick and asphalt. A strong Dutch tell. Elsewhere the
  convention is yellow-and-white paint.
